# Chapter 1 — Theories / Perspectives Worksheet (COMPLETED)

The worksheet asks, for each perspective: **"Describe how it explains our cognitions (thoughts) and behaviors, as well as any important people associated with the perspective."** Below, each perspective gets (1) a **worksheet-ready answer** — the text you'd actually write in the two table cells — and (2) a **deeper explanation** for the test.

Two of the ten perspectives on this worksheet — **Developmental** and **Psychodynamic** — go beyond the eight perspectives in the textbook's Table 1.1, so their entries lean on standard intro-psychology material and are marked [supplemental] where they do.

---

## 1. Biological / Neuroscience

**Focus (worksheet cell):** Thoughts and behaviors arise from **physiological activity** — hormones, genes, brain structures, and brain activity. To explain a behavior, look at what the body and especially the brain are doing. Closely tied to **neuroscience**, the study of the brain and nervous system.

**Important People (worksheet cell):** No single founder in Chapter 1 — modern neuroscientists collectively. (The chapter features neuroscientist Narayanan "Bobby" Kasthuri, who is trying to build a complete 3D map of the human brain's ~100 billion neurons and 100+ trillion connections.) [Supplemental: split-brain researchers Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga are classic names for this row.]

**Deeper:** Question this perspective asks (Table 1.1): *How do biological factors, such as hormones, genes, anatomy, and brain structures, influence behavior and mental processes?* Example: explaining Sam and Anaïs' similarities through the 100% of genes they share. Chapter 2 of the textbook is the foundation for this perspective.

## 2. Developmental *(beyond Table 1.1 — [supplemental])*

**Focus (worksheet cell):** Thoughts and behaviors change in predictable ways **across the lifespan** — this perspective studies physical, cognitive, and social growth and change from infancy to old age, and asks how nature and nurture together shape development, and whether change is gradual or stage-like.

**Important People (worksheet cell):** Jean Piaget (stages of cognitive development in children), Erik Erikson (psychosocial stages across the lifespan), Lev Vygotsky (social/cultural influences on children's cognitive development — he IS in Chapter 1), and G. Stanley Hall (from the People to Know list — a founder of developmental psychology, famous for studying adolescence).

**Deeper:** This is the perspective behind questions like "Is a toddler's brain harmed by screen time?" (the chapter's SpongeBob study is developmental research done with the experimental method) and Vygotsky's claim that parents, teachers, and peers play a critical role in how a child gains knowledge and skills.

## 3. Cognitive

**Focus (worksheet cell):** Behavior is driven by **mental processes** — thinking, memory, language, decision making. To explain behavior, study how people take in, process, store, and retrieve information. The 1950s "cognitive revolution" brought these unobservable processes back into psychology after behaviorism had banned them.

**Important People (worksheet cell):** George Miller (memory research — his famous finding that short-term memory holds about "7 plus or minus 2" items — an important catalyst of the cognitive revolution). [Supplemental: Ulric Neisser named the field; Piaget studied cognitive development.] New branch: **cognitive neuroscience** — physiological explanations for mental processes.

**Deeper:** Table 1.1 question: *How do thinking, memory, and language direct behavior?* Timeline logic for the test: before behaviorism, psychologists studied the mind (Wundt, James); during behaviorism's prime (1930–1950) only observable behavior; in the 1950s the cognitive perspective brought the mind back.

## 4. Psychodynamic *(beyond Table 1.1 — [supplemental])*

**Focus (worksheet cell):** The modern descendant of Freud's psychoanalytic theory: thoughts and behaviors are shaped by **unconscious processes and early life experiences**, and by inner conflicts between our desires and internal restraints — but with far less emphasis than Freud placed on sexual drives, and more on relationships, attachment, and the ways people are unaware of their own motives.

**Important People (worksheet cell):** Sigmund Freud (the root of the tradition), plus the neo-Freudians who revised his theory: Carl Jung, Alfred Adler, Karen Horney, and Erik Erikson. [All supplemental except Freud.]

**Deeper:** How to keep psychoanalytic vs. psychodynamic straight: **psychoanalytic = Freud's original theory and therapy** (unconscious sexual/aggressive conflicts, talk therapy); **psychodynamic = the broader, updated family of theories** descended from it. Many teachers accept "psychodynamic" as the umbrella term for both rows — if your class treats them as one, write Freud for both and note the modern version softens the sexual-conflict emphasis.

## 5. Humanistic

**Focus (worksheet cell):** Human nature is **by and large positive**, and people are naturally inclined to **grow in a positive direction**. Thoughts and behaviors are explained by our drives toward growth, free choice, and self-determination — not by unconscious conflicts or environmental programming. Arose as a rebellion against the rigidity of psychoanalysis and behaviorism.

**Important People (worksheet cell):** Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow.

**Deeper:** Table 1.1 question: *How do choice and self-determination influence behavior?* Legacy: the humanists' early work set the stage for today's **positive psychology** (Seligman & Csikszentmihalyi), which studies happiness, creativity, love, and human strengths. [Supplemental: Rogers → client-centered therapy; Maslow → hierarchy of needs and self-actualization.]

## 6. Behavioral

**Focus (worksheet cell):** Behavior (and personality) is **primarily learned** — through **associations** (classical conditioning), **reinforcers and consequences** (operant conditioning), and **observation**. Only observable, measurable behavior is proper subject matter; the environment (nurture) shapes what we do.

**Important People (worksheet cell):** Ivan Pavlov (classical conditioning — dogs learning to salivate to signals), John B. Watson (founded behaviorism), B.F. Skinner (operant conditioning — behavior shaped by rewards and punishments). [Supplemental: Albert Bandura for learning by observation.]

**Deeper:** Table 1.1 question: *How does learning shape behavior?* The behaviorists are the chapter's strongest **nurture** advocates — though twin studies show nature plays a pivotal role too. Watson threw out consciousness entirely; Skinner admitted mental processes exist but said they can't be studied scientifically.

## 7. Social-cultural (Sociocultural)

**Focus (worksheet cell):** Thoughts and behaviors are shaped by **other people and the broader cultural context** — parents, teachers, peers, social interactions, and cultural norms. What looks like "human nature" often varies across cultures and across groups within a culture.

**Important People (worksheet cell):** Lev Vygotsky (Russian psychologist, 1896–1934 — proposed that parents, teachers, and peers play a critical role in how a child gains knowledge and skills).

**Deeper:** Table 1.1 question: *How do interactions with other people and cultural factors shape behaviors and attitudes?* Key history: in the 1980s, cross-cultural research began revealing that **Western research participants are not always representative of people from other cultures** — so findings can't be assumed universal. Book example: bargaining is expected in many Asian markets but not in US fixed-price stores.

## 8. Evolutionary

**Focus (worksheet cell):** Thoughts, emotions, and behaviors are shaped by **natural selection**: inherited traits that helped ancestors survive and reproduce became more common; maladaptive traits faded. We think and act the way we do partly because those tendencies were adaptive in our evolutionary past.

**Important People (worksheet cell):** Charles Darwin (theory of evolution by natural selection) and David Buss (professor at UT Austin, a founder of modern evolutionary psychology — applies the perspective to personality traits, intelligence, and behaviors like risk-taking).

**Deeper:** Table 1.1 question: *How does natural selection influence thoughts, emotions, and behaviors?* Slide example: birds and beak size (finches); the textbook elaborates — in a drought, big-beaked finches that can crack the remaining tough seeds survive and reproduce, "natural selection right before your eyes" (textbook). Darwin also inspired William James' functionalism, the historical ancestor of this perspective.

## 9. Psychoanalytic

**Focus (worksheet cell):** Behavior and personality are shaped by **unconscious conflicts** — clashes between inner desires (often sexual and aggressive) and the expectations of society, occurring outside of awareness. Early childhood experiences with caregivers set personality development in motion. Gave rise to **psychoanalysis**, the original "talk therapy."

**Important People (worksheet cell):** Sigmund Freud (Austrian physician, 1856–1939).

**Deeper:** Table 1.1 question: *How do unconscious conflicts affect decisions and behavior?* Balance for the test — the **"Freud Problem"**: Freud is psychology's most famous icon, but his theories lack solid scientific support; about 90% of APA members do not practice psychoanalysis, and most science-minded psychologists have distanced themselves from Freudian notions.

## 10. Biopsychosocial

**Focus (worksheet cell):** Thoughts and behaviors are explained by the **interaction of three levels at once**: **bio**logical factors (genes, hormones, brain), **psycho**logical factors (thoughts, emotions, learning), and **social**/cultural factors (relationships, culture, environment). No single lens is enough — behavior emerges from their complex interplay.

**Important People (worksheet cell):** No single founder named in the chapter — it is the integrative framework used by scientists across many disciplines (psychologists studying men's mental health, physicians treating sickle-cell disease). [Supplemental: physician George Engel is usually credited with proposing the biopsychosocial model in 1977.]

**Deeper:** Table 1.1 question: *How do the interactions of biology, psychology, and culture influence thoughts, emotions, and behaviors?* This is the perspective to name when a question says "integrated approach" or "multiple factors." Applying it to Sam and Anaïs: biology (identical genes), psychology (individual coping styles — Sam shakes stress off, Anaïs sits with it), social (French only-child upbringing vs. American household with two older brothers).
